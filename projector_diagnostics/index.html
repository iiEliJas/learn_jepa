What am I looking at?

Each point is a 16-d projector output. The page treats that vector as a sample from one joint distribution and asks two questions:

per dimension: is it N(0,1)? pairwise: are dimensions correlated? SIGReg: does every 1-D slice look Gaussian? T_s = N · Σ_t err(t) · w(t), averaged over slices

A projector can have roughly unit variance in every dimension and still fail the slice test: correlated dimensions or the wrong shape show up in T_s. That is why the page shows the marginals, the correlation matrix and the slice statistic together.

All embeddings are produced in eval mode, so the projector's BatchNorm layers use their running statistics. The batch is sampled from the 2,000-image test subset.

Try this

1. Watch the null. The dashed line is the finite-sample Gaussian expectation, about 1.05. It does not depend on the batch size. The model sits well above it.

2. Change the batch. Raise the batch from 256 to all 2,000. The raw T_s grows with the number of points; the “at N = 256” number stays roughly stable. That scaling is why the report's 2.47 and 2.84 are batch-size statements.

3. Resample. With 256 points, the histograms and the correlation matrix are noisy. Press “Resample batch” a few times and watch which correlations survive. Switch to all 2,000 for a stable picture.

4. Compare modes. With four views, switch between per-view and joint SIGReg. They agree once both are rescaled to the same number of points, but the raw values differ because the joint mode pools four times as many points.

Why marginals are not enough

A dimension can be perfectly standard-normal on its own while the vector is far from N(0, I): rotate a Gaussian cloud and the marginals change, or correlate two coordinates and each marginal stays Gaussian. SIGReg projects onto many directions, so it sees the joint shape that the 16 histograms miss.

The reverse is also true: finite-sample marginals look noisy even when the distribution is right. The KS distance and the Gaussian-null line are there to keep that noise in perspective.

Reading T_s

T_s = N · Σ_t err(t) · w(t), where err is the squared difference between the empirical characteristic function and exp(−t²/2). The raw number grows with the batch, but not purely linearly: the finite-sample null floor stays constant while the signal scales with N. The page reports null + (T_s − null) · 256 / N, so a 2,000-point batch can be compared with the 256-point batches used in training reports without understating the small batch.

The Gaussian null is the finite-grid expectation Σ_t (1 − φ₀(t)²) · w(t), not zero. At t_max = 3 and 17 knots it is about 1.05.
